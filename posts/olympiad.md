# Mathematical Olympiads

## A backstory

In my teenage years, I was able to find the time to learn mathematics in further depth than at school. 
I thoroughly enjoyed repetitive calculations, 
as well as thinking about ideas within a logical system.

Thanks to excellent educators and patrons, there were a number of mathematical competitions I attended.
These motivated me to accelerate my extracurricular studies and apply my knowledge.

Since competitive success also brought fame to my school and my teachers, I was allowed time away from school when there was a competition.
Self-study was easier for me than attending classes.

Anyway, the competitions also gave rise to a community of like-minded students.
I didn't see these friends and acquaintances as my competitors.

In my regard, competitive tests were meant as a motivation to learn more effectively.
For this reason, I dislike the word Olympiad, which makes competitions into a game of strength, like a sport.

Some people saw my participation at every possible competition as a means to personal glory and envied me.
Some others judged my mathematical pastime as a pointless effort in the long run.
Mostly, people congratulated me and praised my achievements, although it wasn't clear to me whether it was my learning or my fame that was being praised.

It was through these achievements that I was admitted, with scholarship, to Trinity College, Cambridge for higher education.
However, it was the problem-solving experience from studying and practicing for Olympiads that brought me to learn university courses and pass final exams.
That being said, I may have preferred more group discussion and social infrastructure in Cambridge.
I would rather have met my peers in a classroom than in a lecture hall.
The mathematical society activities of listening to speaker talks and playing board games numbed my creativity.

I appreciated substantial ideas more than adult contrivances and sheer cleverness.
Hence, I made my way through a PhD in pure mathematics.
I value academic talks in my field, rare as they are, all the better.

## Studying in the context of Olympiads

In the beginning, study involves learning the syllabus and timed practice.
What are the typical problems that appear on the rounds of a qualifying exam?
How quickly and certainly can you answer these questions?
What sorts of ideas do the more difficult, Olympiad-style problems build on?
A beginner will wonder about new ideas and enjoy how much there is to learn.
The difficulty is to experience new things independently.
This is the time when mathematics can feel surprising and fresh.
If you are spoiled by available information and haste, you will feel bored and eventually burn out.

In the middle, study involves keeping up your knowledge.
By regularly repeating ideas you have learned, it becomes possible to solve more complicated problems.
For multiple-choice tests, perfect your sense of precision and only do timed practice.
But can you solve an Olympiad-style problem in one session?
In a session of practice, mix easy, standard questions with a challenge problem.
An intermediate will be satisfied with competence and amazed by long answers.
The difficulty is to keep on going without expecting oneself to improve.
If you are only concerned with scoring points, not with your problem-solving process, you will peak and give up.

In the end, study involves making connections.
Many problems appear simple, yet require ingenuity, the willingness to come up with an idea that works.
If you cling to the ideas that occur to you by habit, you are at a disadvantage, guessing without noticing the problem.
Can you invent or discover ideas that are specifically relevant for a problem?
Are you ready to try an effective technique if it isn't obviously useful?
Having mastered all the prerequisites, work on peculiar problems, regardless of their complexity.
An advanced problem-solver likes intuition and has fun without any concerns at all.
The difficulty is to elaborate as much as necessary.
If you exhaust yourself with awkward and exaggerated reasoning, you won't be able to finish your work.

Especially if you are carried away by the intimidation of sitting in a contest hall, you won't show your true realisation.
There's nothing you can do about how arbitrary it is to be measured at a special time and place.
